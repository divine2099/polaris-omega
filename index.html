Polaris Omega

A cross-chain arbitrage engine with a solver-grade execution layer.

It finds the same asset priced differently on two chains, and checks every trade exactly against the chain before sending it.

Its edge is between chains. There the winner is whoever holds inventory on both sides and prices both correctly, not whoever bids the most to be first into a block.

Built by Jamike Divine, a solo builder working with an AI system. Proven on a public test network; not yet traded with real money.

#01

What it is

Two engines under one roof

The same asset is often priced slightly differently in two places at the same moment. Polaris Omega takes those differences with two engines, and only the second one is the edge.

Engine 1 · one chain

Borrowed and repaid in one transaction

  • Buys on one exchange and sells on another in a single transaction that either profits or undoes itself.
  • The single-chain engine needs no capital of its own: the money is borrowed and repaid inside that same transaction.
  • A race. Whoever lands first in the block wins, so this engine is the entry point and the steady base.
Trading on testnet
Engine 2 · between chains

Inventory on both sides, no race

  • No loan can span two chains, so it needs real capital resting on each side.
  • It does not need to be first. Gaps between chains last many blocks, not one.
  • It competes on pricing both chains correctly, checking every leg exactly and moving inventory cheaply.
Plans live candidatesWaits on capital
The solver layer · inside engine 2

Moving its own inventory, and later filling other people's orders

  • Speaks the order formats of the solver and bridge networks exactly, and uses them to move its own inventory between chains.
  • With enough inventory, the same capital can fill other people's cross-chain orders for a fee, so moving its own money earns instead of costing.
  • That is why it is designed as a hybrid of arbitrageur and solver. The solver side is built and switched off, because it needs capital to front each fill.
#02

How it is built

Full architecture →

Built to run inside the node

Most bots ask a node for data over the network and are always a little behind. This is built to run as an extension inside the node software on Ethereum, Base and Optimism, and beside the node on the same machine on Arbitrum and BNB Chain. Until the servers exist it reads over the network.

Every trade simulated exactly

Before anything is sent, the trade is run against the chain's current state on the same virtual machine the chain uses. If the result is off by the smallest unit, it is not sent.

One small contract, money through doors

A single contract with no stored state carries out each trade. Value moves only through an exchange's own entry functions, checked when the trade is built.

Two keys and a scoped vault

A one-transaction trade takes one key. Anything that settles in two parts needs a second, independent signer. Inventory sits in a Safe, and the trading key gets only a narrow role over it.

Every claim names its check

A ledger records every assumption the system rests on, marked verified, unverified or falsified, with the test that decides it. Its tools are built to tell "nothing was there" apart from "I could not look", because most tools print the same silence for both.

#04

The competition

From published research

On one chain, everyone runs the same trades, and the race to win them hands most of the profit to the house. Between chains the contest is different: nobody wins by outbidding, and that is the contest this system is built for.

~$870MCross-chain arbitrage in one year across nine chains, 2023–24, growing fivefold
2 in 3Of those trades used inventory already in place, settling in a median of 9 seconds
Top 5Addresses did half of all cross-chain trades: few firms, because the barrier is capital
500,000+Price gaps between rollups that nobody took, in six months of one study

How it means to survive. It does not try to beat the leaders at their own trade. It looks for the gaps they do not take, by why a gap exists rather than by the patterns everyone scans, and it carries the risk of one leg settling alone with a priced limit and two keys.

Where it is weaker, said first. The leaders settle in seconds. On the development machine this system priced a couple of hundred blocks behind a fast chain, so a node server is the price of entry, not an extra. Before any capital is at risk, the next milestone replays real-network history to measure which gaps the leaders take, how fast, and what they pay.

Sources: Öz et al., Cross-chain arbitrage: the next frontier of MEV (2025); Gogol et al., Cross-rollup MEV (2024); Wu and Öz, To wait or to probe (2026); EigenPhi (2023).

#05

Where the thinking has moved

November 2026

The first version found the trades everyone looks for. The plan has moved past that: find gaps by their cause, prove them on history, and run everything as one loop.

Finding

Look for why a gap exists

Price gaps have a few causes: one asset under two rules, markets on different clocks, thresholds that force a trade, frictions that keep a gap open, identities that break between chains. Search by cause, and a known strategy becomes a label on what was found.

Designed
Evidence

Prove it on real-network history first

Replay past blocks through the same exact simulator and label every opportunity: who took it, how, and what they paid. Real-market evidence with no capital at risk.

Next milestone
Learning

A model that can only miss

History trains a model that suggests where to look. The simulation and the safety checks decide, so a wrong suggestion costs a missed trade, never a bad one.

Designed
Coverage

The whole market, vetted continuously

Protocols are discovered from the chains and quarantined until checked. Any upgrade to one in use stops trading on it until reviewed.

Upgrade watch: liveDiscovery: designed
Capital

Funding forms that never leave the owner

A vault per chain is built. Borrowing through that vault's role, delegated credit, and a partner's own vault with the split paid on chain are designed.

#06

What holds it back

None of it is code
Critical
The machine

The development machine cannot host a node, so it reads the chain over the network. On Base, with a block every fifth of a second, it was measured a couple of hundred blocks behind against a 10-block safety window. That figure was taken on an older, slower access plan and is being measured again, but no network plan reaches a node's speed. The fix is a bare-metal server per chain, about $456/month for the first and ~$1,600/month for four. The server layout is available on request →

Critical
Capital

The single-chain engine needs only gas, and that is not where the edge is. The cross-chain engine, the solver fills and later market making all need working capital on each chain. That part is built, plans real candidates from live data, and stops at exactly this point.

High
Chain access for sending

Private routes that put a trade straight into a sequencer or a block builder instead of the public queue, some of them paid. Until the nodes exist it also reads through a paid plan at a small tier, which a long live run would outgrow.

High
The AI bill

One person builds this with an AI coding system, and its plan tier sets the pace. A better plan is the cheapest way to make everything move faster.

#07

Milestones

Timed from when each one's need is met

Two tracks. The first proves the whole system works together before any money is at risk: every part switched on, trained on real history, and tested under stress against rivals. The second puts it on real networks, one need at a time. Each milestone says how anyone can check it is done, and times run from the day the thing it waits on is in place.

  1. Proven on a test networkDone

    Trades found, simulated, signed and landed exactly as predicted; a trade through a scoped vault; the two-key path driven to the send.

    Check: every receipt is public on the test network.
  2. A replay of real-network historyNext · needs no funding

    A stretch of one real network's past blocks replayed through the exact simulator, every opportunity labelled with who took it and what they paid.

    Check: a written report with ranges, reproducible from public block data.
  3. Every part working togetherUnder way · needs no funding

    Every component called by the running program rather than only by its tests, each boundary between two of them tested with real code on both sides, and every kind of trade wired end to end as a dry run.

    Check: an integration suite with a test for every boundary, and the capability map showing every component switched on.
  4. Trained on historyAfter the replay · needs no funding

    The labelled history becomes training data. A model that ranks where to look is kept only if it beats a simple baseline on history it has not seen, and the risk coefficients are estimated from real outcomes instead of defaults.

    Check: the held-out comparison, and each coefficient with its sample and its refusal when the sample is too thin.
  5. Tested under stress, all parts at onceAfter training · needs no funding

    The unmodified engine runs inside a simulated multi-chain world built from real state, against rival bots, through six scenarios: steady markets, a volatility spike, a chain going offline, a gas spike, hostile tokens and failing solvers, and lopsided inventory. The simulation is checked against the replayed history.

    Check: every scenario passing, and ten thousand simulated trades with no loss of principal.
  6. Beside a node on one chain+2 weeks from the server

    The system running beside its own node and pricing inside the safety window on the live chain.

    Check: the measured lag, published.
  7. First real-money trades+6 weeks from the server

    Single-chain first, from a small set of wallets declared in advance.

    Check: every transaction published net of gas, failures included, for the first thirty days.
  8. First cross-chain trades+8 weeks from capital in the vault

    Inventory on two chains in a vault its owner controls, every leg co-signed by two independent keys.

    Check: both legs of each trade on chain, and a public count of how often a leg settled alone.
  9. Filling other people's ordersAfter that record

    The solver side fills other people's cross-chain orders from the same inventory, starting with a venue that needs no one's permission. Some venues also require a registered company.

    Check: the fills on chain, in the same public record.
  10. The other chainsOne at a time

    Each chain added the same way, each with its own record.

    Check: that chain's record.
